A bug safari is a slow, deliberate walk around the yard or park with one job: find as many different insects as you can and actually watch them. It takes ten to thirty minutes, needs nothing but a kid and a patch of ground, and works in any season. The version that always works: pick a start point, turn over one thing, look at one flower, check one damp corner, and count your legs. Five different bugs is a good safari. Ten is a great one.

That is the whole thing. Everything below is what makes it go from "we looked at some bugs" to a kid who lies flat on the grass for twenty minutes because the ant is carrying something and they need to know where it is going.

## What you need (almost nothing)

Nothing is required. Bugs are already there. But three cheap things make it better:

- **A clear jar or cup.** Not to keep anything, just to hold a bug still for thirty seconds so a kid can look at it properly before it goes back. Put a leaf in so it has something to stand on.
- **A magnifying glass.** The single biggest upgrade. A woodlouse at normal size is a grey dot. Under a cheap magnifier it is armoured, segmented, and has more legs than anyone expected. Kids who were bored are suddenly not.
- **Something to draw on.** A notebook or the back of an envelope. Drawing a bug is how a kid actually looks at it, because you cannot draw legs you have not counted.

No app, no kit, no printable. A stick to lift things with is useful and free.

## Where to look: the six places bugs always are

A safari with no plan means wandering around a lawn seeing nothing. Bugs live in specific places, and once a kid knows the six spots they will find something every single time.

1. **Under things.** Rocks, logs, flowerpots, a piece of bark, the doormat. Lift, look, put it back gently. This is where woodlice, beetles, centipedes and worms live, and it is the most reliable spot on the list.
2. **On flowers.** Anything in bloom. Bees, hoverflies, butterflies, and the tiny beetles that live inside the petals. Stand still for a full minute and something will land.
3. **Along edges.** Where the lawn meets the fence, the path meets the soil, the wall meets the ground. Ants run edges. So do spiders and ground beetles.
4. **Anywhere damp.** The shady corner, under the tap, the base of a downpipe, the compost. Slugs, snails, and everything that eats them.
5. **Dead wood.** A rotting log or an old stump is an apartment block. Look in the cracks and under loose bark.
6. **Up, on the leaves.** Turn a leaf over. Caterpillars, aphids, ladybirds and their larvae, leaf miners leaving squiggly trails. Most kids never look at the underside of a leaf until someone tells them to.

Bonus spot for the brave: **outside with a torch after dark**, especially on a warm evening. Moths, beetles, and a completely different set of spiders. Same yard, different safari.

## What you will find, by season

The same yard gives you a different safari four times a year, which is the reason it never gets old.

- **Spring.** Ladybirds waking up, the first bees on the first flowers, ants rebuilding, worms near the surface after rain. Caterpillars appear as soon as the leaves do. This is the easiest season for a first safari.
- **Summer.** Peak everything. Butterflies, grasshoppers you can hear before you see, dragonflies near any water, beetles on every flower head. Look in the evening when it is cooler and the bugs are slower.
- **Autumn.** Spiders. Big orb webs strung across paths at head height, and the spiders sitting in them. Also the last butterflies, crane flies bumbling about, and beetles under the fallen leaves.
- **Winter.** Quieter but not empty. Lift a log and there are woodlice and centipedes carrying on as if nothing happened. Ladybirds huddled in window frames. Snails sealed up under pots. A winter safari is a hunt for who is hiding where, and that is a good question for a kid to ask.

## The three safari rules

Say these before you start and they will hold all afternoon.

1. **Look, do not squish.** Every bug goes back where it came from. The jar is a viewing window, not a cage.
2. **Put the roof back.** Whatever you lifted, you lower again, gently. The rock is somebody's house.
3. **Hands off the stripey ones.** Bees, wasps, and anything bright red or yellow gets watched from a step away. Everything else is fair to gently scoop with a leaf.

Three rules a four-year-old can repeat back to you. That is the right number.

## Make it fit the age

- **Toddlers (2 to 4).** One rule: find something moving. Lift one rock together, look, put it back, big reaction. Two minutes is a full safari. Stop while they still want more.
- **Little kids (5 to 7).** Give a number and the six places. "Find five different bugs, one from under a rock, one from a flower." Counting legs is the game. They will want the jar.
- **Bigger kids (8 and up).** Make it a study. Which spot had the most bugs? Why? What was the ant carrying? Come back tomorrow, is the same spider in the same web? This is the age where a magnifier and a notebook turn a safari into the beginning of actually knowing things.
- **Mixed ages.** The little one holds the jar and does the counting. The big one does the finding and the drawing. Both report back to you.

## When the safari is over

The yard does not change, but the kid does. After two or three safaris they stop needing the six-spot list. They lift the flowerpot on the way to the car without being asked. They notice the web across the gate. That is the actual point, and no worksheet does it.
