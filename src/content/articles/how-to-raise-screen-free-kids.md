Raising screen-free (or lower-screen) kids comes down to four things that reinforce each other: build screen-free habits into fixed points in the day so nobody has to negotiate them, keep ready-to-go alternatives within arm's reach so boredom has somewhere to go, model the behavior yourself because kids copy what they see more than what they're told, and make the real world genuinely more fun than the screen instead of just banning the screen. Start with one anchor (screen-free mornings, or dinner, or the hour before bed), stack a few easy alternatives behind it, and let it become the unquestioned normal. That beats a dramatic zero-screen crackdown every time.

Because the goal here isn't a perfect, screen-free childhood, and chasing one will just make you feel like you're failing. **The goal is a kid whose default, when nothing's scheduled, is to go do something real.** Screens don't lose that battle by being banned. They lose it by being less interesting than the fort in the living room.

## Build habits, not bans

A ban is a fight you keep re-winning. A habit just happens. Aim for habits.

- **Anchor screen-free time to something that already happens every day.** "No screens until after breakfast," or "screens off while the sun's up," ties the rule to a moment, so it stops being a daily decision and starts being just how your house works. When the limit is the clock rather than your mood, kids push back way less.
- **Expect a rough few days.** A kid used to filling every gap with a screen won't know what to do at first, and they'll tell you so, loudly. That's not the plan failing, it's the old habit dying. Ride it out.
- **Keep some screens.** A movie night, a video call with grandparents, a game you play together. Low-screen that leaves room for the good stuff is a rule a family can keep for years. Zero-screen usually isn't.

## Keep ready-to-go alternatives within reach

The tablet's superpower is that it's always right there, fully loaded, zero setup. The most useful thing you can do is give the real world the same head start.

- **Stock a boredom bin.** A box of low-setup gear (chalk, string, a magnifying glass, tape, cards) makes the answer to "I'm bored" a two-second point at the box, not a scramble for ideas.
- **Have a few missions you can hand over on the spot.** Vague invitations ("go play") are easy to refuse; a specific quest is a puzzle a kid wants to solve. [The Stick Sword Hunt](/app/quest/the-stick-sword-hunt) sends them out to find the perfect stick, [The Bug Safari](/app/quest/the-bug-safari) turns a sidewalk crack into an expedition, and [Pebble Tower Champion](/app/quest/pebble-tower-champion) is a stacking challenge that needs a handful of rocks and nothing else.
- **Leave the good stuff visible.** A screen wins partly because everything else is in a closet. Blocks on the shelf, art supplies on the table, a fort half-built in the corner. What's in sight gets used.

## Model it yourself

This is the uncomfortable one, and it's the one that matters most. Kids read what you do, not what you say. If your reflex in every quiet moment is to reach for your phone, "screens are what you do when nothing's happening" becomes the family truth no matter what the house rules say.

- **Have your own screen-free anchors, out loud.** Phone in a drawer at dinner, a book you're actually reading, a walk without earbuds. When your kid sees you choose the real world, it stops being a punishment reserved for children.
- **Do the alternative with them, at least sometimes.** A parent who occasionally builds [The Secret Fort](/app/quest/the-secret-fort) or lies in the grass playing [Cloud Zoo](/app/quest/cloud-zoo) is teaching, in the most direct way there is, that this stuff is worth an adult's time. And you'll slip up; "let's both put our phones down" models something better than pretended perfection.

## Make the real world the better offer

The lasting fix isn't restriction. It's competition. You want a kid who'd rather be outside, and that only happens when outside is set up to win.

- **Add a goal.** Plain outside can look like plain concrete. A mission gives it a point, and a point is what a screen has that an empty afternoon doesn't.
- **Let it be a little wild.** Getting muddy, building something rickety, climbing the low wall. The real world's edge is that it's physical and slightly unpredictable, so don't sand all of that off.
- **Add a small payoff.** Screens hook kids with constant little rewards, so borrow the trick: a points system, a sticker chart, a "you did it" at the end. A goal plus a reward turns a chore into a game.
